FRC Router Feeds & Speeds Explainer

Every input here is a number you can look up — off your tool's datasheet, a machinability table, or your machine's spec sheet. Nothing is expressed relative to some other tool, and nothing is a fudge factor. The calculator shows its work so you can check it.

⚠️
These are starting points, not guarantees. Verify workholding, chip evacuation, tool quality, and machine condition before you cut.

Where these numbers come from

Tool vendors publish a cutting data table for each end mill series. It is usually one row per workpiece material class. Here is what a typical one looks like, for a 4 mm 3-flute AlTiN-coated cutter:

∅4.00 mm · 3 flutes · LOC 8.00 mm · OAL 50 mm

Illustrative table in the standard vendor format. Use your own tool's published numbers — do not cut from this one.

Reading it into the fields below

On the datasheetField hereNotes
∅ / Mill diameter Tool → Diameter Convert mm to inches: mm ÷ 25.4. A 4.00 mm cutter is 0.1575″.
Number of flutes Tool → Flutes Multiplies feed directly. It does not change the RPM limit.
LOC / length of cut Tool → Length of cut Hard cap on depth per setup. Caps ap automatically.
ISO codes (P/M/K/N/S/H) Tool → Rated ISO groups Checked against the material. Geometry and coating are matched to a material class, so a mismatch is more than a speed question.
vc (SFM or m/min) Tool → Datasheet SFM Pick the row matching your material. m/min × 3.28 = SFM. Leave blank to use the material table instead.
fz (in/tooth or mm/tooth) Tool → Datasheet fz Take the value for your diameter. mm ÷ 25.4 = inches. Leave blank to use the material table instead.
ae max / ap max Operation → overrides Usually given as a multiple of diameter. The operation presets already pick sane values; override when the sheet disagrees.
Coating (AlTiN, TiCN, ZrN…) Tool → Coating Only used when you leave Datasheet SFM blank, to scale the material table's surface speed.
💡
No datasheet? Plenty of tools ship with nothing but a product listing. Leave Datasheet SFM and Datasheet fz blank and the material table fills them in from published machinability data, scaled to your tool's diameter and adjusted for its substrate and coating. That is a starting point rather than a guarantee, so start at the conservative end and listen to the cut.

Where the other two groups come from

  • Material — a machinability table. Machinery's Handbook, a tooling vendor's material chart, or the supplier's spec for the exact alloy. You need surface speed, chipload as a percentage of diameter, and how deep the material tolerates being cut.
  • Machine — your machine and spindle spec sheets. RPM range and max feedrate come off the controller or VFD configuration; spindle power off the nameplate (kW × 1.34 = HP). Rigidity is the one judgement call: light = hobby gantry or extruded aluminum frame, medium = welded-steel gantry router, heavy = knee mill or VMC.
